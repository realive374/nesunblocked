@PART|PART FOUR|THE OLD CANTEEN HALL
@CH|28|Day One
The old canteen hall had not been used for eating in about fifteen years.

It was enormous and it had a high ceiling and it had very good acoustics by accident, which is the best way for a room to have them. They put a long table at one end for the Panel, and two smaller tables facing it, and then they put out chairs for residents, and they put out two hundred chairs because Mr Pell said two hundred would be plenty.

Nine hundred people came on the first morning.

They stood along the walls and they sat on the floor in the aisles and they filled the gallery at the back that nobody had been up to in years, and at eleven minutes past nine Ada Olumide walked in, looked at the crowd for a long moment, and said:

"Right. Nobody is sitting on the stairs. Everything else I can live with."

She sat down.

"This is a review under clause thirty," she said. "It is not a trial. Nobody here is accused of a crime and nobody is going to be punished. I say that now because I have already heard four people in the corridor use the word 'trial' and one person use the word 'prosecution,' and both of those words are wrong, and if I hear them in this room I will correct them every single time, which will get boring for all of us."

She put on her glasses.

"The question before this Panel is narrow. It is not whether Ordinance 11 is a good idea. I want to be extremely clear about that, because nine hundred of you have come here this morning to find out whether Ordinance 11 is a good idea, and you are going to be disappointed.

"The question is whether, on the fifteenth of April, this council properly considered the submission that was actually before it. That is all. If it did, the decision stands, however much anybody dislikes it. If it did not, I may quash it and send it back to be done again.

"Appearing for the petitioner — " she looked up — "Mr Jacob Reyes."

"Yes, ma'am."

"Mr Reyes, you are nine."

"Yes, ma'am."

"Clause thirty does not mention age."

"No, ma'am."

"I have read it forty times looking for one," said Ada Olumide. "It isn't there. I am satisfied that you are properly appointed and I will hear no further argument about it from anybody." She turned her head. "Appearing for the council, Mr Voss."

Mr Voss stood up.

He was a large, courteous, extremely competent man of about fifty who had come down from the administration and who had, everybody agreed afterward, one of the hardest jobs anybody has ever been handed in Playcare. He had to argue against a nine-year-old in front of nine hundred people, and he had to do it properly, because doing it improperly would have been worse.

He did it properly. I want that on the record. Mr Voss never once talked down to Jacob, never once played to the room, and lost anyway, and behaved impeccably about it.

"Ma'am," he said.

"Mr Voss, your case in one sentence."

"The council considered the submission, found the alternative it proposed to be the status quo ante that had already failed, and said so in writing. Consideration does not require agreement."

"That is a good sentence," said Ada Olumide. "Mr Reyes. Yours."

Jacob stood up.

He had a box on the table in front of him. It was not the shoebox — the shoebox was under his chair, because Bobby had told him that bringing a literal shoebox into a hearing would make people laugh and he had accepted the note. It was a proper document box with a lid.

"Ma'am," he said, "the council wrote down its reasons. That was honest of them and I want to say so first." He put one hand on the box. "But the reasons they wrote down are the answer to a question we did not ask. We did not ask them to let children decide. We asked them to decide more — to sit down with us and watch it and tell us what was wrong with it. Their minute says our proposal was 'supervised co-viewing,' and then says that was the prevailing approach before November."

He looked up.

"It wasn't. Nobody was doing that. Nobody has ever done that in Playcare, not once, and I can prove it, and that is my whole case."

There was a certain amount of noise in the hall.

Ada Olumide let it run for about four seconds and then said, "No," in a conversational voice, and it stopped.

"Mr Voss," she said. "You may open."
@CH|29|Mr Voss Opens
Mr Voss opened for two hours and eleven minutes and he was very good.

He took them through it properly. He took them through the September proposal — Ms Delight's six pages, which he read large sections of aloud, including finding number three, because he was not afraid of it. He took them through the October vote, seven to two. He took them through the ordinance, the Wardens, the detentions, the numbers.

He did not pretend the ban had worked.

That was the thing that made him dangerous, and Jacob knew it within about eleven minutes and spent the entire first morning revising his plan.

"The council does not say the ordinance succeeded," said Mr Voss. "The council has never said that. What the council says is that every alternative available to it fails worse, and that the standard being applied to it here is one that no policy in the history of this community could meet.

"My learned friend will show you that children copied episodes out by hand. That is true. It is also, with respect, exactly what one would expect, and it is not an argument. When you prohibit a thing, some people will want it more. That is a cost. It is a known cost. It is on the ledger. Against it sits a library that went from nine visitors an evening to a hundred and forty, and stayed there, for seven months.

"My learned friend will show you a resignation letter from a Warden. It is a moving letter and the council has read it with care. It is also one letter, and there are ten Wardens still serving.

"And my learned friend will, I expect, put a great deal of weight on the fact that the petitioners asked to be consulted and were not.

"Ma'am, I want to address that directly, because it is the only part of this petition that troubles me."

He put his notes down, which he had not done once in two hours.

"The council did not consult the children of Playcare before passing Ordinance 11. That is a fact and I do not dress it up. The council's position is that it was not required to, that it would not have changed the outcome, and that a body which consults on every measure never passes any.

"But I will say this on my own account and not on instructions." He paused. "If the Panel finds that the council should have asked, the council will not appeal that finding. We would accept it. We would find it uncomfortable and we would accept it.

"What we cannot accept is the proposition that because eleven hundred children want something, the council must give it to them. That is not review. That is the end of the council."

He sat down.

Ada Olumide wrote for a while.

"Thank you, Mr Voss," she said. "That was exceptionally fair and I have noted what you said on your own account and I will come back to it." She looked up. "Mr Reyes. It is twenty past eleven. Would you rather open now or after lunch?"

"Now, please, ma'am."

"Go on then."

Jacob stood up.

He was four feet and one inch tall. There was a small riser behind his table that Bobby had built him out of two crates and a plank, and which was, in the end, the only concession anybody made to his height in eleven days.

"Ma'am," he said, "I'm going to be much shorter than Mr Voss."

"That is a low bar," said Ada Olumide. "He was very long."

There was some laughter. Mr Voss laughed too, which is the kind of thing that tells you who somebody is.

"Mr Voss said the council's reasons were honest," said Jacob. "They were. And he said consideration doesn't mean agreement. It doesn't. I'm not going to argue with either of those, because they're true, and I've learned this year that if you argue with the true bits you lose the room."

He opened the document box.

"So here is the only thing I'm going to say this morning, and then I'm going to spend the rest of this hearing proving it.

"The council's minute says our alternative was 'the prevailing approach prior to November.' Those are their words. That sentence is the hinge of their whole reasoning — because if our alternative had already been tried and had already failed, then rejecting it without discussion is obviously proper consideration, and I lose, and I should lose.

"Ma'am, that sentence is not true.

"Not 'arguable.' Not 'a matter of opinion.' Not true, in the way that a date is not true or a number is not true. Nobody in Playcare was doing supervised co-viewing before November. There was no such programme, there was no such policy, there was no such practice in any dormitory, in any classroom, or in any staff meeting minute in this community's history. I have looked. I have looked for four months.

"The prevailing approach prior to November was that eleven hundred children were left alone with an hour and a screen and nobody ever once sat down next to them."

He closed the box.

"They didn't reject our proposal," said Jacob. "They mistook it for a thing they already had. And you can't consider a thing you've mistaken for something else."

Day two through day five was evidence, and evidence is slow, and I am going to move through it quickly, which is a betrayal of how much work it was.

Jacob put in a hundred and nine documents.

He did it himself, one at a time, standing on a plank across two crates, saying "Exhibit nineteen, ma'am, the note from Mr Day of the twenty-second of November, one page, handwritten, and I have the original and a copy for Mr Voss," four hundred times, in a completely flat voice, for four days.

It was the least dramatic thing that has ever happened in the old canteen hall. Attendance dropped to about two hundred by Wednesday. Several accounts of this case skip these four days entirely.

They are the four days that won it.

Because what Jacob was doing, patiently, one sheet at a time, was building a thing that nobody could argue with: a complete documentary record of what Playcare had actually done about children and screens, for the eleven years anybody had records for, with every single gap in it left visible.

There was no co-viewing programme in 2014. There was no co-viewing programme in 2015. There was a proposal in 2016 by a dormitory supervisor named Mrs Achebe which was discussed for nine minutes and shelved for cost. There was nothing in 2017. There was nothing in 2018. There was a line in a staff development plan in 2019 that used the phrase "media literacy," which Jacob put in himself, against his own interest, and then produced the minute showing it had never been funded.

"I could have left that one out," he said to Bobby on the Wednesday evening, eating chips on the steps outside the hall.

"Why didn't you?"

"Because Mr Voss would have found it." Jacob wiped his hands. "And then everything else I said would have been worth nothing. You get one of those, Bobby. You get one thing you hid, and then you're just a person who hides things."

He put in the library circulation figures — and he put them in whole, including the good ones, including the hundred and forty, which was Mr Voss's best fact, and Mr Voss looked up sharply when he did it.
