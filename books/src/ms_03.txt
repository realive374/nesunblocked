@CH|18|The Wardens
Playcare had never had Wardens before.

That is worth sitting with for a moment. The word turned up on a cream-coloured notice on a Thursday afternoon as though it had always been there, as though there had always been Wardens and everybody had simply not needed them until now, and within about nine days there were eleven of them and they had jackets.

They were not monsters. I want to establish that early, because it is going to become steadily harder to remember.

They were, for the most part, people who already worked in Playcare and had been given an extra duty and a small increase in their pay. Mr Halloran, who did the crosswords, was a Warden. So was Mrs Okonkwo of the hedge. So was a young woman named Fenn who ran the climbing wall and who had taken the job, she told people, specifically so that somebody decent would have it.

They had a clipboard each and a laminated card with the text of Ordinance 11 on it, and they had been given exactly one afternoon of training, which had consisted of a man from the council reading the ordinance out loud and then asking if there were questions.

There had been eleven questions. He had been able to answer four.

The first week was quiet.

That surprised everybody, including the council, and it should not have. A prohibition does not produce crime in its first week. In its first week it produces a sort of stunned politeness, where everybody waits to see what the new shape of the world is going to be and nobody wants to be the one who finds out.

The rec hall screens went dark on Friday morning — not restricted, dark; the council had decided that rather than filter them, which finding number three had shown to be useless, they would simply remove the browsers from them entirely, and so eleven hundred children arrived after dinner to find eight large blank rectangles and a laminated card.

There was no riot. There was no protest. Sixty kids stood in front of the blank screens for a while and then went and played football, and a member of the council who had come down specifically to watch reported back that the transition had been "smooth, and frankly a lesson in how much of this we over-thought."

The second week was also quiet.

In the third week, a Year Five called Tomas — the one whose arm Bobby had sat with for six hours — was found in a storage room off the gymnasium with a tablet, and the tablet was held, and Tomas was detained for two hours, and his mother was written to.

He was the first.

There were four hundred and ninety-two more.

The detention room was the old music room, which had been chosen because it had no windows and had not been used for anything since the piano went. They put eleven chairs in it and a table and a jug of water, and a Warden sat at the table with the clipboard, and children sat in the chairs for between one and four hours depending on what number offence it was, and then they went home.

It was not cruel. Nobody was hurt. Nobody was shouted at.

It was, in fact, extremely boring, and that was the entire design, and it was — for the first month — reasonably effective.

Here is what the council did not model.

When you take the eight screens out of the rec hall, you do not remove the hour. The hour is still there. Eleven hundred children still have one hour between dinner and lights-out, and that hour has to go somewhere, and it turns out that an hour with nothing in it is not a neutral substance.

By the middle of the fourth week, the following things were true in Playcare:

The queue for the two functioning table-tennis tables was, at its peak, forty-one people long.

Somebody had started a fight club behind the laundry. It was a very bad fight club. It lasted nine days and produced one sprained thumb and was broken up by Hoppy Hopscotch, who was horrified, and who made everybody involved run laps with her until they stopped enjoying it, which took under four minutes.

Three separate groups of children had independently invented the same game, which involved a ball and a wall and an extremely complicated scoring system, and all three groups believed they had invented it, and there was a period of about a week where this was the single most contentious issue in Playcare.

And the reading room, which had averaged nine visitors an evening for as long as anybody could remember, was averaging a hundred and forty.

Ms Vance, who ran it, did not know what to do with herself. She ran out of the easy chapter books in eleven days. She put in a request for more and the council approved it in one meeting without debate, which was the fastest the council had ever approved anything, and several members mentioned it in their own private notes as the thing that made them feel best about the whole business.

You could, if you wanted to, tell this story as a success.

A lot of people did, for about five weeks. There are minutes from the council meeting of the eighteenth of November in which a member says, on the record, "Reading is up fifteen hundred percent. Whatever else is true, that is true." And it was true. It stayed true.

But by the eighteenth of November the other thing had also started, and the other thing was not in the minutes, because the other thing did not happen in rooms where minutes were taken.
@CH|19|The Whisper Network
It started, as far as anybody can reconstruct it, with a girl called Priya Nandakumar and a piece of paper.

Priya was ten. Priya was the one who had worked out the double-press on the photocopier. Priya had, in her own estimation, been personally robbed by Ordinance 11 of something she was good at, and she took it about as well as you would expect.

What she did was this.

She wrote out an episode.

The whole thing. From memory. Every line, every yell, every beat, in a lined exercise book, with the character names in the margin like a play script, and where she could not remember the exact words she wrote what she thought they had been and put a small (?) after it, because Priya had standards.

It took her four evenings.

Then she lent it to somebody.

And then somebody copied it.

By the first week of December there were — and this is Jacob's count, and Jacob's counts are good — a hundred and nine handwritten episode transcripts in circulation in Playcare, of which sixty-two were in exercise books, thirty-one were on loose paper, and sixteen were on the backs of other things, including four on the reverse of the cream-coloured Ordinance 11 notices, which had been taken down off noticeboards for the purpose and which Jacob regarded, correctly, as the single funniest fact in the entire case.

They did not call them transcripts. They called them "the books."

You could borrow a book. There was a system. There was, by January, an actual lending system with an actual ledger kept by an actual eleven-year-old, with due dates, because the moment children are given a scarce resource they will invent a library within about six weeks, and they will invent late fees within eight.

The late fee was a biscuit.

And here is the thing about the books, the thing that nobody in the council anticipated and that Ms Delight, when she finally understood it, took very badly indeed:

The books were worse.

Not worse quality — worse. Priya had a good memory but she did not have a perfect one, and neither did the forty children who copied her, and every copy drifted a little, and the drift was not random. The drift went one direction. Every time a child copied out an episode they made the rude bits slightly ruder, because that was the part they remembered best and the part that got the laugh when you read it out behind the recycling bins.

By February there were versions in circulation that contained words the actual channel had never used in its life.

There was a version — Jacob has it, it is Exhibit 34, and it is genuinely shocking — in which a character says something so far past anything the original had ever done that when it was read out in court the clerk had to stop and the judge had to ask her to skip to the next line.

Ms Delight sat through that reading with her hands flat on the table in front of her.

She had banned a channel.

And the ban had produced, inside four months, by the ordinary action of eleven hundred bored children with pencils, a strictly worse version of the thing she had banned, circulating in handwriting, uncensorable, untrackable, and — because it was made of paper and paper is not a device — completely, perfectly legal under every word of Ordinance 11.

"You cannot hold a notebook," Jacob said in court, "because the ordinance says devices."

He was nine. He had read the ordinance forty times. He had found the hole in it in the first week and had said nothing about it for five months, on purpose, and when the council's lawyer asked him why he had said nothing, he said:

"Because I was collecting."
@CH|20|CatNap
CatNap was detained on the ninth of January.

This was, in every practical sense, an accident, and the Warden involved spent a long time afterwards wishing she had done almost anything else.

Here is what happened. Fenn — the young woman from the climbing wall, the one who had taken the job so that somebody decent would have it — was doing a walk-through of the east dormitory corridor at about nine at night, which was after lights-out, and which was a routine thing that Wardens did.

And she heard a sound from a linen cupboard.

The sound was a small, muffled, tinny burst of noise, and then a child's laugh, immediately smothered.

Fenn opened the cupboard.

Inside the cupboard, sitting on a stack of folded sheets, were four children and CatNap. The four children had an ancient scratched-up media player between them, the kind that does not connect to anything and simply holds whatever somebody put on it four years ago, and on the media player was an episode of SML, and CatNap — who was supposed to be, whose entire function in Playcare was to be, the one who put children to sleep — was the one holding it.

Fenn said, "Oh, no."

CatNap looked up at her.

He did not say anything. CatNap did not say much at the best of times, and this was not the best of times. He simply looked at her with his big flat purple face, and then very slowly, very deliberately, he put his paw over the screen, which was the single least effective act of concealment in the history of Playcare, and which Fenn described later as "the saddest thing I have ever seen anybody do."

"CatNap," said Fenn. "Come on. You know what this is."

"They couldn't sleep," said CatNap.

"I know."

"Nobody can sleep." His voice was a low rumble. "Do you know how many of them can't sleep? Since November? I do. I'm the one that knows." He looked at the four children, who had gone absolutely white. "They had an hour. Every night. It was loud and it was stupid and it wore them out and then they went to bed. I don't have anything to put in the hole, Fenn."

Fenn stood in the doorway of a linen cupboard for a long time.

Then she wrote it down, because that was her job, and because she had taken the job so that somebody decent would have it, and a decent person who does not write it down is just a person who has decided the rules are for other people.

She wrote all five names.

She detained CatNap for four hours the following afternoon, because it was his first offence but he was an adult, technically, in the way that the Critters were technically adults, and the ordinance said without exception of age.

He sat in the old music room with no windows in a chair that was much too small for him, with a jug of water he did not touch, for four hours.

And at some point during those four hours, word got out.

I do not know how. Jacob does not know how either and it bothers him to this day. But by two in the afternoon there were about forty children standing in the corridor outside the old music room, not doing anything, not shouting, not blocking anybody — just standing there, in a corridor, quietly, for four hours, so that when CatNap came out he would not have to walk anywhere alone.

Fenn, sitting at the table with the clipboard, could hear them through the door.

She resigned as a Warden that evening.

Her resignation letter is in the record. It is three sentences long.

"I took this post believing that the job would be done badly by somebody else if I did not do it well. I no longer believe that a well-done version of this job exists. I have detained the person whose job is to help children sleep, for helping children sleep, and I was correct to do it under the ordinance, which is the entire problem."
@CH|21|The Hole in the Hour
Fenn was right about the hole and she was not the only one who noticed it.

DogDay noticed it first, actually, back in November, before the books and before the linen cupboard. DogDay noticed everything about the mood of Playcare, in the way that a person who is professionally cheerful has to notice everything, because cheerfulness that does not read the room is just noise and DogDay was not noise.

He put it in a note to the council on the twenty-second of November.

It is a short note, handwritten, and it is Exhibit 19, and it says:

"I am not writing about the rights or wrongs of the ordinance, which is not my business. I am writing about the eight o'clock hour, which is my business.

Before, the hour had a shape. It was loud and it was daft and I did not enjoy it myself, and at nine o'clock the kids were tired out and went to bed.

Now it does not have a shape, and a thing I have learned in this job is that children do not do nothing. They cannot. If you take the shape out of an hour they will put a shape in it, and they will use whatever is lying around, and what is lying around is each other.

Reading is up, and that is good, and I am glad. It is about a hundred and forty of them. There are eleven hundred.

I would like to request a budget for the other nine hundred and sixty."

The council discussed it on the sixth of December.

They approved forty pounds.

It is in the minutes. Item nine. "Request from D. Day re. evening provision. Approved £40 from contingency." And underneath, in the same minute, item ten, which is the purchase of a second clipboard and a further set of laminated cards for the Wardens, at a cost of one hundred and twelve pounds.

Jacob put those two lines on one page, side by side, and enlarged them, and that page is Exhibit 20, and when the council's lawyer objected to it on the grounds that it was "argumentative," the judge looked at it for a while and said, "It's a photocopy of your own minutes, counsel," and let it in.

DogDay spent the forty pounds on skipping ropes.

He bought eleven of them. He taught about ninety children to use them properly over the course of a fortnight, in the way that DogDay taught things, which was extremely enthusiastically and with a great deal of demonstrating, and for about three weeks in January there was a skipping craze in Playcare that was genuinely, unambiguously lovely.

Then it stopped, the way crazes stop.

And the hour went back to being a hole.

By February the hole had a texture to it that everybody could feel and nobody could name. Hoppy described it, in the only interview she ever gave about that winter, as "like the sound a fridge makes, that you don't hear until it goes off." CraftyCorn said her art club attendance went up and the art got worse. Bubba Bubbaphant, who was the most careful of all of them with a sentence, said this:

"The children were not miserable. I want to be precise, because people afterwards wanted them to have been miserable and they were not. They were fine. They were bored, and being bored is survivable and occasionally productive, and I am on record as having said so before all this started.

What changed was not their happiness. What changed was that eleven hundred children learned, over five months, that there was a thing they all wanted, that all of them knew where to get, that none of them could have, and that the adults had made a rule about without asking any of them a single question.

I do not think the council understood what they were teaching. They thought they were teaching that SML was bad. They were teaching a much larger and much more portable lesson, and it was this: the rule is not about you, and the rule will not be explained to you, and if you want the thing you must simply get better at not being seen.

Nine hundred and sixty children took that lesson. They were very good students. They took it in about six weeks."
@CH|22|What Bobby Did
Bobby BearHug did not get better at not being seen.

Bobby BearHug went completely the other way, which is what nobody expected, least of all Bobby.

For the first eight weeks of the ordinance she did nothing at all. She stopped watching, because she was told to, and she did not join the lending library, and when Priya offered her a book Bobby said no thank you in a voice that made Priya not ask again. She was not sulking. She was doing something much more strenuous than sulking, which was that she was trying, very hard, at nine years old, to believe two things at once.

Thing one: Ms Delight was kind. She had been kind in the office. She had crouched down. She had said not one thing.

Thing two: this was all downstream of nine seconds in that office, and children were sitting in a windowless room, and CatNap had put his paw over a screen.

You cannot hold those two for very long. Adults cannot do it. Bobby held it from October to the middle of January and then it came apart in about four minutes in a corridor.

She was walking past the old music room on the day of CatNap's detention. She was not part of the forty; she had not heard. She turned a corner and there they were, forty kids standing along a wall doing absolutely nothing, in total silence, and she stopped, and she said, "What's happening?"

And a small kid at the end of the line, who was about six, said, "We're waiting for CatNap."

Bobby said, "Why?"

And the small kid said, "So he doesn't have to walk on his own."

Bobby BearHug stood very still in that corridor.

Then she went and stood at the end of the line.

She stood there for three hours and forty minutes.

She was the one, when the door finally opened, who got to him first — she was closest, she had positioned herself, she had been thinking about it for three hours — and she hugged him, and CatNap, who was easily four times her size and who had not been touched by anybody in a fortnight, more or less folded.

That is the moment, if you want one. People have looked for the moment for years. The books get it wrong; the version that went round afterwards has Bobby making a speech, and she did not make a speech, Bobby BearHug made almost no speeches in her entire life.

She hugged a purple cat in a corridor.

And then she turned around, with him still leaning on her, and she said to the forty kids standing along the wall, in a perfectly ordinary voice, the eleven words that ended up on about four hundred pieces of paper in Playcare by the end of that month:

"This is silly. Let's go and tell somebody it's silly."

Now.

I need to tell you what she actually did next, because "let's go and tell somebody" is the kind of thing children say, and almost nothing follows it, and what followed this one was four months of extremely unglamorous work.

Bobby did not organise a protest.

She went and asked for a meeting.

She went to the council office above the canteen, on the sixteenth of January, at ten past four in the afternoon, with her hair brushed, on her own, and she knocked on the door and she said to the clerk that she would like to request five minutes at the next council meeting, please, and the clerk — a decent man named Mr Pell who had been doing that job for nineteen years — explained very gently that council meetings were for residents over eighteen.

Bobby said thank you very much and went away.

She came back on the seventeenth.

She had, by then, read the council's constitution. Jacob had got her a copy. It was forty-one pages and she had read all of it, at nine, over one evening, with Jacob explaining the hard bits, and she had found that while speaking rights were restricted to residents over eighteen, the submission of a written proposal was open to "any resident," full stop, with no age attached, which was almost certainly a drafting error that had sat there unnoticed for thirty years.

She pointed this out to Mr Pell.

Mr Pell read the clause. He read it again. Then he took his glasses off and said, "Well, I'll be damned," and then apologised for saying that in front of a nine-year-old, and Bobby said it was fine, she had heard worse, which was true, and which was arguably the origin of this entire book.

"You can submit a proposal," said Mr Pell. "That's not five minutes at a meeting. I can't give you five minutes. But a proposal has to be read into the record and it has to be minuted and it has to receive a response in writing."

"How long does it have to be?" said Bobby.

"There's no minimum."

Bobby thought about that.

"Is there a maximum?"

And Mr Pell, who had been doing his job for nineteen years and had never once been asked that question, looked down at a nine-year-old bear in the doorway of his office and said:

"No."

@CH|24|The Proposal of Bobby BearHug
It took eleven weeks.

Bobby submitted her proposal to the Playcare Council on the fourth of April. It was, by a distance, the longest document ever submitted to that body, and Mr Pell had to get a second folder for it.

It was two hundred and six pages.

Two of those pages were Bobby's.

The other two hundred and four were signatures.

Here is what she had done, and I want to go slowly, because this is the part that the fast version of the story leaves out entirely, and it is the part that actually mattered.

She had asked every single child in Playcare the same four questions.

Not a petition — she had been extremely firm about that with Jacob, who had wanted a petition, because a petition was tidy. Bobby said no. Bobby said a petition is a thing where you tell people what they think and then they agree with it, and she did not want to tell anybody what they thought. She wanted to ask.

The four questions were:

1. Do you watch it? (Y / N / I used to)
2. Do you think the grown-ups were right to be worried? (Y / N / A bit)
3. Has the ban worked? (Y / N)
4. What would you have done instead? (Write anything. Or nothing.)

She asked eleven hundred and four children.

She did it in the eight o'clock hour, which was the hole, which was the one hour of the day when eleven hundred children had nothing to do and were therefore, it turned out, extremely willing to spend four minutes answering questions from a bear who was standing there being sincere at them.

It took her eleven weeks because she insisted on doing every single one herself, in person, which Jacob told her repeatedly was insane, and about which he was correct, and about which he was also — he said this later, under oath, and he was not a sentimental person — "completely and entirely wrong, and I want that in the record."

The results were not what anybody expected.

To question one, eight hundred and ninety-one said yes or I used to.

To question two — do you think the grown-ups were right to be worried — six hundred and eighty-two said yes or a bit.

Sit with that number. More than three in five children in Playcare, asked in private by somebody they trusted, with no adult in earshot, said that the grown-ups had a point.

To question three — has the ban worked — one thousand and ninety-one said no.

And question four is the reason that document is two hundred and six pages long, because question four said write anything, and eleven hundred children did.

Some of them wrote one word. Some of them drew. One of them, aged six, wrote "make it less loud," which Jacob framed. Tomas, the first child ever detained, wrote a page and a half and it is the single most moving thing in the entire exhibit list and I am not going to reproduce it here because it belongs to him.

But the most common answer — by a very long way, in a hundred and forty different handwritings, in spelling that ranged from immaculate to heroic — was a version of the same nine words.

Ask us.

Watch it with us.

Tell us what's wrong with it.

That was the proposal. Bobby's two pages at the front said, in summary:

"We do not think the council is bad. Six hundred and eighty-two of us think you were right to worry, which is more than half, and we would like you to know that before you read anything else, because nobody has asked us what we think about any of this and we would like the first thing we say to be that.

We think the ban has not worked. One thousand and ninety-one of us think that. We have attached why.

We would like you to do the thing on page four instead.

We have all signed it. That is what the rest of the pages are."

Mr Pell read the covering pages standing up in his office with the door open.

Then he sat down.

Then he read the whole thing, all two hundred and six pages of it, which took him until nine o'clock at night, and at some point around page ninety he called his wife and told her he was going to be late.

The next morning he took it to the council chair and said, "You need to read this today."

The council read it on the fifteenth of April.

They rejected it eight to one.
@CH|25|Eight to One
The vote was not close and the debate was not cruel and that is what made it so hard to get past.

Nobody in that room said a single unkind thing about Bobby BearHug. The minutes are in the record and you can read them and it is genuinely difficult to find a villain. Several members said the document was remarkable. Two of them said, on the record, that they had been moved by it. The chair said — and she meant it, everybody who knew her says she meant it — that it was "the most impressive piece of work this council has received from any source in my eleven years."

And then they voted it down eight to one.

The reasoning is in item four of the minutes and it is only three sentences and it is, I think, the most important three sentences in this book:

"The council accepts the finding that Ordinance 11 has not eliminated the material. The council does not accept that this is grounds for repeal, as no measure available to this council would eliminate the material, and the alternative proposed — supervised co-viewing and structured discussion — was the prevailing approach prior to November and produced the circumstances that led to the ordinance. The council notes with respect that the petitioners are children and that the question before us is precisely whether children's own assessment of what is good for them should be determinative, which it cannot be."

Read that last clause again.

The question before us is precisely whether children's own assessment of what is good for them should be determinative, which it cannot be.

That is not stupid. That is not evil. That is, in most circumstances, in most rooms, about most subjects, completely correct, and every adult reading this knows it is correct, and so did Jacob, who read that clause eleven times and then put it down and said a word that I am not going to tell you either.

@CH|26|The Thing About Playcare Nobody Uses
Playcare had a court.

Like the council, most kids did not know this, and unlike the council, most adults did not know it either, because it had been used four times in thirty years and three of those were about drainage.

It was in the constitution, at clause 30, on page thirty-three, in a section headed REVIEW, and what it said was this:

"Any resident may petition the Review Panel on the ground that a decision of the council was made without proper consideration of a submission properly before it. The Panel shall consist of one person appointed by the council, one person appointed by the petitioner, and one independent chair agreed by both, who shall have the casting voice. The Panel may uphold, vary, or quash the decision under review."

Jacob found it at twenty past eleven at night.

He had been reading the constitution front to back for the fourth time by torchlight under a blanket, which is exactly as ridiculous as it sounds, and he got to clause 30 and he read it and then he sat up so fast he hit his head on the bunk above him.

The key word — and he saw it instantly, and he was right about it, and eleven weeks later a judge agreed with him — was consideration.

Not decision. Not outcome. You could not petition because the council had decided something you did not like. That would be absurd; that would mean every drainage decision in Playcare could be relitigated forever by anybody who was annoyed.

You could petition because they had not properly considered what you actually put in front of them.

Which is exactly, precisely, demonstrably what had happened, and it was demonstrable because they had written their reasons down in their own minutes, in three sentences, and those three sentences answered a proposal that did not exist.

Jacob got up at twenty to midnight and went and stood outside the girls' dormitory and got a passing eleven-year-old to fetch Bobby, which took nine minutes and caused a certain amount of comment.

Bobby came out in a dressing gown.

"Jacob, it's midnight."

"Clause thirty," said Jacob.

"What?"

"Clause thirty. Page thirty-three." He held out the constitution with his thumb in it and his hand was shaking slightly. "Bobby, we can make them do it again. We can make them do it in a room, out loud, with somebody watching, where they have to actually answer the thing we actually asked."

Bobby took the book.

She read clause 30 in a corridor at midnight in a dressing gown.

Then she said: "Who's the chair?"

"Somebody both sides agree on."

"Who do we pick for ours?"

And Jacob said, "You pick me."

Bobby looked up.

"You're nine."

"It doesn't say an age." Jacob's jaw was set. "I read the whole clause eleven times, Bobby. It says 'one person appointed by the petitioner.' That's the entire sentence. There is no age in clause thirty. There's no age in the submission clause either, which is how you got your proposal in, and that's not a coincidence — whoever wrote this thing thirty years ago either forgot about children entirely or thought about them very hard, and I genuinely cannot tell which."

"They'll say no."

"They'll try."

"Jacob, they'll absolutely say no — "

"Then they'll have to say no out loud," said Jacob, "in front of an independent chair, with a reason, in writing. And Bobby — " and here, for the first and very nearly the last time in this entire story, Jacob Reyes smiled — "I have four hundred and eleven pieces of paper and I have been waiting two and a half years for somebody to make me say why."

The petition was filed on the twenty-second of April.

It was filed in the name of Bobby BearHug, resident, aged nine, and it named as respondent the Playcare Council, and the case was listed in the register, by the clerk, in the register's own antique formula, as:

BEARHUG v. THE PLAYCARE COUNCIL

Nobody called it that.

Within about four days everybody in Playcare was calling it something else, and the something else was unfair in about four different directions, and it stuck completely, and it is what the case is called in every account of it including this one.

They called it Playcare v. Delight.

Ms Delight was not the respondent. Ms Delight was not a party. Ms Delight had submitted a proposal in September, which the council had adopted in October, and after that point she had precisely as much formal standing in the matter as any other resident, which is to say none.

She found out what people were calling it on the twenty-sixth of April, in the staff room, from Mr Adeyemi, who did not realise he was the one telling her.

She put her cup down very carefully on the counter, which was a thing she did.

Then she said, "I see," and went to Room 4 and taught a lesson about the water cycle, and about nine of the twenty-six children in that room later said they could not tell that anything at all was different about her, and two of them said they could, and one of those two was a small serious boy in the second row who kept his pencils in order of length.

The independent chair took three weeks to agree.

The council proposed four names. Jacob rejected all four, in writing, with reasons, which nobody had ever done before and which caused a genuine procedural crisis because the constitution did not say what happened next. Jacob proposed two names. The council rejected both.

On the fourteenth of May, Mr Pell — who was by this point, and he would deny this, entirely on the children's side — suggested a name that neither party had thought of.

She was a retired magistrate named Ada Olumide who had come to Playcare eleven years earlier to look after her sister and had stayed on after her sister died, and who lived in the staff village four doors down from Ms Delight, and who had spent the entire last seven months telling anybody who would listen that Ordinance 11 was, in her professional opinion, "the worst-drafted document I have read since I retired, and I have read a planning application from a man who wanted to keep a horse."

The council did not want her.

Jacob did not entirely want her either, and he explained why to Bobby in a way that Bobby found deeply confusing at the time and understood completely about four months later.

"She already agrees with us," he said.

"That's good."

"That's terrible."

"Jacob — "

"If she already agrees with us and she finds for us, everybody in Playcare says it was fixed," said Jacob. "Every single person. Forever. And then the ban comes back in two years and we can't do this again." He pushed his glasses up. "I don't want to win a thing that people can say was fixed. I want to win it so hard that nobody can say anything."

He accepted her anyway, because the alternative was another three weeks, and because Bobby said, "Jacob, we have to actually do it, it's been seven months," and she was right.

Ada Olumide accepted the appointment on the sixteenth of May.

Her first act as chair was to write to both parties, in identical letters, and the letter said, in its entirety:

"I have been appointed chair of the Review Panel in the matter of Bearhug v. The Playcare Council. I am aware that I have expressed opinions about Ordinance 11 in this community and I have offered both parties the opportunity to object to my appointment on that basis. Neither has. That door is now closed and I will not hear about it again.

I will hear this properly. By properly I mean that both sides will be heard in full, that evidence will be tested, and that I will not be hurried by anybody's feelings, including my own.

The hearing will begin on the ninth of June in the old canteen hall. It will be open to any resident who wishes to attend.

Both parties should note that I intend to sit for as long as this takes."

It took eleven days.
