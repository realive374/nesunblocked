Ms Delight had written them out twice. The first version had nine rules on it and she had thrown it away at about eleven at night, because she had read it back and heard the sound of a person losing an argument. The second version had four.

1. Screens on in this house between 5:00 and 6:30 p.m. Not before. Not after.

4. If you think a rule is stupid, say so to my face and I will listen. Behind my back does not count and will not work.

@CH|13|How They Did It
They did it with the school library.

I am going to explain this properly, because when it came out it was the single most humiliating forty seconds of Ms Delight's life, and because the explanation is genuinely, I think, the funniest thing in this entire book, and because if I skip it you will imagine something much more impressive than what actually happened.

Playcare's Schoolhouse had a library. The library had six computers. The six computers were for research, and they were locked down extremely thoroughly — no video, no games, no messaging, a filter list as long as your arm, the whole apparatus. Ms Delight had personally sat on the committee that approved the filter list. She had argued for a stricter one and lost.

The six library computers could not reach SML. They could not reach anything like SML. The filter was, by every account including Jacob's, genuinely excellent.

But the library also had a photocopier.

And the photocopier, which had been bought in a hurry four years earlier to replace one that caught fire, was a modern one, and modern photocopiers are not photocopiers. Modern photocopiers are computers with a scanner bolted on. This one had a screen on the front of it, a proper colour touchscreen, for choosing your paper size and your collation options and so on.

And in the corner of that screen, for reasons that nobody has ever adequately explained, and which I suspect come down to a salesperson somewhere adding a feature to a list, there was a small button.

The button said HELP.

And the HELP button opened the manufacturer's support site.

In a web browser.

A full one.

"It took me four minutes," Marlow said afterward, on the stand, in front of everybody, in the flattest voice a twelve-year-old has ever produced. "I wasn't even looking for it. I was trying to print a thing for history."

The photocopier browser did not have the school filter on it, because the school filter was installed on the six library computers, by name, by a technician, in an afternoon. Nobody had put it on the photocopier, because nobody had ever once thought of the photocopier as a thing that could go on the internet, because it was a photocopier.

So: at 4:47 p.m. on that Wednesday — the first day of The Arrangement, the day it had worked so beautifully in the front room with the door open — Marlow Delight stood in the Playcare Schoolhouse library, in front of a photocopier, and watched eleven minutes of SML on a four-inch screen at the lowest volume the machine could produce, with his ear about six inches from the speaker grille.

It was, by his own description, "really bad. The screen's tiny and it's green and it keeps asking about paper."

And he did it anyway.

And that is the part that eventually broke Ms Delight in half, and it was not the rule-breaking. It was the eleven minutes on a green four-inch photocopier screen with a bad speaker. It was the sheer, grinding, cheerful determination of it. She could have understood defiance. Defiance is a thing you can negotiate with.

What she could not get past was that her son had wanted it enough to stand up.

By Friday there were four of them doing it.

By the following Tuesday the number was somewhere around thirty, the queue for the photocopier after last bell had become a known social event, and a Year Six named Priya had worked out that if you pressed HELP twice quickly the machine forgot to log you, which meant the usage report that went to the office every Monday showed a photocopier that had made eleven copies in a week and been switched on for forty hours.

Somebody in the office looked at that report.

That somebody was not Ms Delight.

That somebody was the Schoolhouse business manager, a careful man named Mr Adeyemi, who assumed the machine was faulty, and who filed a maintenance ticket, and who mentioned it in passing, in the staff room, on the morning of the twenty-sixth of September, to a colleague.

"Forty hours and eleven copies," he said, stirring his coffee. "Either it's broken or the children have found something."

And Ms Delight, who was two feet away making tea, put her cup down very carefully on the counter and said:

"Say that again."

@CH|16|The Proposal
Playcare had a council.

Most kids did not know this. Most kids assumed Playcare ran the way weather runs — that the lamps came on because they came on, that the food arrived because it arrived, that somewhere there was probably a grown-up in charge but that the grown-up was more of a rumour than a person.

There was, in fact, a council. It met on the first and third Monday of the month in a room above the canteen with a long table in it, and it had nine members, and it was — this is important — genuinely, boringly, legitimately in charge. It set the lamp hours. It approved the menu. It had, four years earlier, approved the purchase of a photocopier.

Ms Delight was not on the council.

She was, however, entitled to submit a proposal, because any resident of Playcare over the age of eighteen was, and roughly four people a year did, and they were almost always about the orchard.

She submitted hers on the twenty-ninth of September.

It was six pages long. It was typed. It had a bibliography.

I have read it. It is, I am sorry to say, extremely good.

That is the part people cannot stand about this story and it is the part I refuse to leave out. When people imagine the SML ban they imagine something scrawled and furious, a document written at two in the morning by a woman who had lost her grip. What she actually submitted was a calm, structured, meticulously argued six-page policy paper with a summary at the top, four numbered findings, three options with costs attached, and a recommendation.

The summary said:

"A substantial majority of Playcare's 1,100 children are consuming, for approximately one hour per day, a media product whose comedic structure is built on the transgression of exactly the behavioural norms this community spends its entire budget attempting to instil. The content is not illegal, not violent in any meaningful sense, and not, in my professional judgement, produced with any malice whatsoever. That is precisely why every existing tool has failed. I have tried the household tool. It failed in thirty-one hours. I am not proposing this because I believe children are fragile. I am proposing it because I have measured the size of the thing and it is larger than a fridge."

Finding number three said:

"Filtering does not work and cannot be made to work. The Schoolhouse filter is professionally maintained and was defeated by a twelve-year-old via a photocopier in four minutes. Any technical measure this council funds will be defeated at a similar cost. I include this finding against my own interest, because it eliminates Option A, which is the option this council will want."

And the recommendation — Option C — said:

"A prohibition. Total, community-wide, enforceable. Not a filter, not a guideline, not an advisory to parents. A prohibition with a penalty attached to it, because an unenforced prohibition is a guideline with a costume on."

At the bottom of page six, she had written, in her own hand, underneath the typing:

I am aware of what I am asking for. I would ask the council to notice that I have not asked for anything smaller only because everything smaller has already been tried by me personally and has already failed.

The council read it on the third Monday of October.

They debated it for two hours and eleven minutes.

They passed it seven to two.

"Jacob, how much stuff do you have?"

And Jacob Reyes, nine years old, small for nine, sitting behind the recycling bins with a kind bear who had been crying for twenty minutes, said the sentence that started the second half of this book:

"Four hundred and eleven pieces. In a shoebox. Under my bunk." He picked up his bag. "Do you want to come and look at it?"
