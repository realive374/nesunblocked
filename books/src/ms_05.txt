Nobody slept much on the night between day ten and day eleven.

Jacob did not sleep because he had a closing speech to write and no idea how to write it. He had written one in March and it was four pages long and it was about photocopiers and clipboards and library figures, and it was a good speech, and at about nine o'clock that evening he read it through twice and then put it in the bin.

Bobby found him at half past ten sitting on the floor with an empty page.

"You threw it away."

"It was about the wrong thing."

"Jacob, it took you three weeks — "

"It was about how the ban doesn't work." He rubbed his eyes under his glasses. "Bobby, that stopped being the argument about four days ago. Voss conceded it. Delight conceded it in September in her own paper. I'm the only person left in that building who thinks the ban working is an interesting question, and I've been carrying a speech about it around for three months like an idiot."

"So what's the argument?"

"The argument is what they did to page four," said Jacob. "It has always been what they did to page four. Everything else is scenery."

He pulled the page toward him.

"And I don't know how to say it in a way that doesn't sound small," he said. "Because it is small, Bobby. That's the horrible thing. We're not asking her to say the ban was evil. We're asking her to say that eight people in a room above a canteen read a document badly. That's it. That's the whole thing. That's what we've spent seven months on."

Bobby sat down on the floor next to him.

"Jacob," she said, "can I say something you won't like?"

"Probably."

"It's not small."

"Bobby — "

"It's not." Bobby pulled her knees up. "I asked eleven hundred and four kids four questions and I'm the only person in Playcare who did that, so I'm going to tell you what I found out and you're going to let me." She counted on her fingers, the way he did, which she had picked up off him without either of them noticing. "Six hundred and eighty-two of them said the grown-ups were right to worry. Right? More than half. They agree with her. They agree with Ms Delight."

"I know, I've read — "

"And a thousand and ninety-one of them said the ban didn't work." Bobby looked at him. "Jacob, that's the same kids. That's not two groups. That's the same kids saying 'you were right to worry' and 'this isn't the answer' at the same time, and nobody has ever once asked them a single question, and so nobody in Playcare knows that about them."

Jacob went still.

"Say that again," he said.

"That's the same kids — "

"No, the other bit. Nobody knows that about them."

Bobby said it again.

Jacob picked up the pen.

Across Playcare, in a small house at the end of a row in the staff village, Ms Delight was also awake.

She was at the kitchen table with the green notebook in front of her, and she had been sitting there since about nine, and she had not written anything.

At twenty past eleven Marlow came down for water and found her there.

They looked at each other for a second.

"You're up," he said.

"So are you."

He got his water. He stood at the sink with it for a while, and then, instead of going back up, he sat down opposite her, which he had not done voluntarily in about a year and a half.

"Was it bad?" he said. "Today. I wasn't there."

"No."

"Mum."

"It was not bad," said Ms Delight. "It was accurate. Those are different and I want to be precise about it, because I have spent seven months being imprecise and it has cost this community a great deal of money."

Marlow turned his glass around on the table.

"Was it about Gran?"

Ms Delight looked up sharply.

"Why do you ask that?"

"Because you've got her notebook out," said Marlow, "and it's not her notebook, it's yours, but it's the same kind, and you buy the same kind, and I've known that since I was about eight and never said anything."

Ms Delight put her hand flat on the cover of the green notebook.

"Yes," she said. "It was about your grandmother."

"What did she do?"

And Delia Delight told her twelve-year-old son, at half past eleven at night at a kitchen table, about a carpet and a television and a red certificate and a woman who sat down instead of standing up, and Marlow listened to the whole thing without interrupting once, which was not like him at all.

When she finished he said: "So she did rule four."

"What?"

"On the fridge. Rule four. Come and argue with me to my face and I'll listen." He shrugged. "She did rule four. Thirty years ago. On a carpet."

Ms Delight sat with that.

"Yes," she said.

"So why'd you go to the council?"

And she said the truest and worst thing she said in the entire seven months, and she said it to her son and not to a hearing, which is probably where it belonged:

"Because rule four is very hard, Marlow, and it has to be done every single week forever, and it does not scale, and I am one person and there are eleven hundred children in this place and I could not think of a way to sit on eleven hundred carpets." She pressed her fingers against her eyes. "So I looked for something that would do it all at once. And there isn't one. There is no such thing. There has never been such a thing and there never will be, and I am forty-one years old and a professional and I went looking for one anyway, because I was tired, and because the alternative was admitting that the only thing that works is the thing that takes twenty years."

Marlow said, "That's not a bad reason."

"It is a bad reason."

"It's not a bad reason, Mum, it's just not a good enough one."

Ms Delight laughed.

It was a completely unexpected sound and it startled both of them. It was short and it was wet and it was, several people would have been amazed to learn, a real laugh — the first one in that kitchen in a long time.

"No," she said. "No, it is not a good enough one."

She opened the green notebook.

"Go to bed, Marlow."

"Are you going to write in that?"

"I am going to write a letter," said Ms Delight, "and it is going to be the hardest thing I have ever written, and I would like to do it alone."

She had four pages of handwriting in front of her and she read them out in full, in a completely level voice, without looking up once, which took nineteen minutes, and which is available in the Playcare register to anybody who wants it.

I am going to give you the parts that mattered.

"This is the ruling of the Review Panel in the matter of Bearhug v. The Playcare Council. My colleagues have indicated their positions to me in writing and I will state them at the end.

"First, what this case is not about.

"It is not about whether SML is good or bad. I have not watched it. I have deliberately not watched it, and I want that noted, because had I watched it I would have formed a view and my view is irrelevant. Nothing in this ruling should be read as an opinion about the content of that channel.

"It is not about whether Ordinance 11 is wise. That is a matter for the council.

"It is not about Ms Delia Delight, who is not a party, who has behaved throughout these proceedings with a candour I have rarely seen, and who has, in my judgement, been treated extremely unfairly by this community for seven months in a manner having no basis in anything she actually did.

"She wrote a paper. She was entitled to write a paper. Any resident may write a paper. "Second, the question I do have to decide.

"Clause thirty asks whether a decision was made without proper consideration of a submission properly before it.

"I find that it was.

"I want to be precise about why, because I am conscious that this ruling will be read by people who were not here.

"The council's minute of the fifteenth of April characterises the petitioner's alternative as 'the prevailing approach prior to November.' That characterisation is the load-bearing element of the council's reasoning. If it is correct, the council's decision is plainly proper — one need not deliberate at length before declining to return to a policy that has just failed.

"It is not correct.

"Mr Reyes put before me a hundred and nine documents spanning eleven years. I have read all of them. They establish, and Mr Voss to his very great credit did not contend otherwise, that no programme of supervised co-viewing has ever existed in Playcare. Not in a dormitory, not in a classroom, not in a funded plan. The nearest approach is a proposal by Mrs Achebe in 2016, which was shelved on cost after nine minutes.

"The prevailing approach prior to November was not supervision. It was absence. Eleven hundred children had an hour and a screen and no adult, and that was not a policy, it was a vacancy, and the council has mistaken a vacancy for a policy and rejected a proposal on the strength of it.

"I do not find bad faith. I find eight tired people at the end of a long meeting reading a very long document quickly and seeing in it the thing they expected to see. That is an ordinary human failure and I have done it myself.

"It is still a failure to consider, and clause thirty does not have a good-faith exception, and I decline to invent one this afternoon.

"Third. Remedy.

"Mr Reyes asked me to quash the decision of the fifteenth of April and remit it. Mr Voss submitted that quashing would place thirty years of council decisions in doubt.

"I reject Mr Voss's submission and I want to explain why, because it was his best point and it deserves an answer.

"Nothing in this ruling concerns the length of the council's reasons. A council may give one sentence. A council may, in an appropriate case, give none. What a council may not do is give reasons that respond to a document other than the one it received. That is a narrow ground, it is a rare ground, and in thirty years of this constitution it has been made out precisely once, this afternoon.

"The petition of the fourth of April stands before the council undetermined. The council shall determine it afresh, and shall give reasons that engage with what is actually written on page four, and shall do so within twenty-eight days.

"But I have sat in this room for eleven days, and I am going to say one thing that is not a finding and has no legal effect whatsoever, and the council is free to ignore it entirely.

"Nine hundred people came here on the first morning. Six hundred of them were children. They sat through four days of documentary evidence, which I would not wish on an adult. They were quiet. They were orderly. Not one of them had to be removed.

"It is a very generous number.

The council had twenty-eight days and took twenty-six.

They did not meet above the canteen. The chair, whose name was Mrs Iyabo Fenton and who had been on the losing end of a great deal of public feeling for seven months, made a decision in the first week that changed everything afterward and that she has never taken any credit for.

She moved the meeting into the old canteen hall.

And she opened it.

Not to speak — the constitution still said eighteen, and Mrs Fenton was not a woman who bent a rule to look good. Anybody could come and sit and watch the council deliberate, in public, in a room with four hundred chairs in it, which had never been done in thirty years.

Four hundred people came. Then six hundred. By the fourth session they had run out of chairs again.

The council read page four out loud, line by line, over four sessions, and argued about it in front of everybody. They argued about the cost. They argued about who would staff it. Mr Tembo, who had voted for the ordinance in October and against Bobby's proposal in April, spent forty minutes taking page four apart on practicality grounds, and he was right about most of it, and it was the most useful forty minutes of the entire month.

On the fifth session they invited Bobby BearHug to sit at the table.

They could not let her speak. They could not amend the constitution in a fortnight. So Mrs Fenton did something that Jacob later described as "the single most creative act of procedural mischief I have ever seen and I include everything I did," which was this:

She put Bobby at the table with a pen and a pad, and she announced that the council would take a five-minute recess every time Bobby wrote something down and held it up.

Bobby held it up fourteen times.

They were not speeches. Bobby BearHug never made a speech in her life. They were things like "ask CatNap about 8 o'clock" and "Priya knows which bits are the bad bits, she wrote them out" and, on the fourth one, which caused a nine-minute recess and which is in the minutes as a facsimile, in round careful handwriting:

"you keep saying who will do it. ask us. we will do it. thats what the whole thing said"

The council's determination was published on the eleventh of July.

It is nine pages long. It engages with page four line by line. It is, by a distance, the most thorough document that body has ever produced, and the first two pages are an apology that does not contain the word sorry anywhere in it and is much better for that.

The determination did three things.

It rejected page four as drafted, on cost and staffing grounds, exactly as Ada Olumide had said it was entitled to do.

It adopted a modified version — the Eight O'Clock Programme — funded at eleven hundred pounds a year, which was more than three times what Bobby had asked for, because Mr Tembo's forty minutes of taking it apart had established that Bobby had costed it far too cheaply, which is the most Bobby BearHug thing in this entire book.

And, in paragraph forty-one, on the last page, it repealed Ordinance 11.

Not because of the hearing. The determination is very careful about this and Mrs Fenton was very careful about it in public afterward: the Panel had made no order touching the ordinance and the council was not acting under compulsion.

It repealed it because of Exhibit 98.

CatNap's exercise book. Seven months of tally marks made by somebody who was not trying to persuade anybody of anything. Forty children a week who could not sleep, then three hundred.

Paragraph forty-one says:

"The council adopted Ordinance 11 on the basis that a prohibition would reduce harm. The council now has before it seven months of contemporaneous data, compiled by a person with no interest in the outcome, showing a sevenfold increase in sleeplessness among the children of this community over the period of the prohibition.

Whatever else may be said for the ordinance, it has made three hundred children a week unable to sleep, and the council did not know this, and the reason the council did not know this is that the council did not ask.

Ordinance 11 is repealed with immediate effect. All detention records arising under it are expunged. The council places on record that no child detained under this ordinance did anything wrong.

The council further places on record its regret to Ms Y. Fenn, whose resignation letter of January stated the position with more clarity than this council has managed in nine pages, and to whom the post of Coordinator of the Eight O'Clock Programme is offered should she wish to have it."

Fenn took the job.

She took it on one condition, which she put in writing, and which the council accepted without discussion:

"I will do it if Bobby BearHug and Jacob Reyes are on the committee. I don't care how you square that with the constitution. That's the condition."

They squared it by inventing a thing called the Residents' Panel, which had no age limit in it because Mr Pell drafted it and Mr Pell had learned his lesson, and which has met on the second Tuesday of every month ever since.

There had been more than a question; there had been a petition, in July, got up by some adults in the staff village, asking that she be moved to another post, and it had forty-one signatures on it, and it was the single ugliest thing that happened in Playcare that entire year.

It was stopped by a counter-petition with one thousand and nine signatures on it, which was organised in four days by a nine-year-old bear who had recently become extremely good at getting eleven hundred children to write their names on things.

Bobby did not tell Ms Delight she was doing it. Ms Delight found out from Mr Pell, who put both documents on her desk side by side without saying anything, which was the kindest available way to do it.

She sat with them for a long time.

Then she went and found Bobby, in the rec hall, on a Thursday afternoon in August, and said:

"May I sit down?"

Bobby moved over.

They sat on the low wall outside the rec hall for a while without saying anything, which is a hard thing for adults and an easy thing for Bobby BearHug, who had spent her whole life being comfortable in other people's silences.

"I want to say something," said Ms Delight, "and I am going to do it badly."

"Okay."

"On the fifteenth of September last year I asked you a question in my office four different ways until you answered it."

"I remember."

"I have thought about that morning approximately nine hundred times." Ms Delight was looking straight ahead at nothing. "I have thought about it as the moment I found out. I have thought about it as the beginning of everything that went wrong. Mr Reyes stood up in a hearing and told nine hundred people it was the only time in the entire story an adult did the right thing, and I have thought about that too, every day since, and I do not entirely agree with him."

"Neither do I," said Bobby.

Ms Delight turned her head.

"No?"

"You did do the right thing," said Bobby. "You asked me and you kept asking till I said a true thing. That bit was good. I've never minded that bit. Everybody thinks I mind that bit."

"But?"

"But then you went away," said Bobby.

Ms Delight said nothing.

"That's the bit," said Bobby. "You asked me the hard question in the nice room and I told you the truth and then you said off you go and I went back to class and I never saw you again. Not properly. You did the first half." She swung her feet. "Your mum did the second half. That's the difference. That's the whole difference, that's it, that's the entire thing everybody's been arguing about for a year. Your mum asked the question and then she sat there."

Ms Delight put her hand over her mouth.

It was, several people who saw it from across the rec hall agreed, the slowest and most horrible movement any of them had ever seen. Her hand came up in stages, like it was arriving from a long way off, and when it got to her mouth she pressed it flat and held it there.

Bobby BearHug looked at her for a second.

Then she reached over and took the hand and pulled it down and said, "You don't have to do that. I can't understand you with your hand there."

And Ms Delight made a sound that was not a word.

"Ms Delight?"

"Yes, Bobby."

"Are you okay?"

"No," said Ms Delight.

"Okay," said Bobby. "That's allowed."

They sat there for another while.

"I would like to come to the Tuesdays," said Ms Delight eventually. "I have not come. I have been standing outside the rec hall on Tuesdays since the twenty-eighth of July and I have not gone in, and last week Marlow went in and I did not, and my twelve-year-old is braver than I am, which I have been aware of for some time and which I do not enjoy."

"So come in."

"Bobby, there are eleven hundred children in that hall and every single one of them knows my name."

"Yeah," said Bobby.

"They will look at me."

"Yeah," said Bobby. "For about a minute. And then something'll happen on the screen and they'll stop, because they're nine, and nine-year-olds cannot look at anything for more than a minute, which is the entire reason we're all in this mess."

Ms Delight laughed.

It was short and it was wet and it was, on the whole, real.

"Will you sit with me?" she said.

And Bobby BearHug, who had given out eleven thousand hugs and had once counted thirty before breakfast and had never in her entire life been asked to do anything harder than this, said:

"Yeah. Course. I'll save you a seat. I'll do the one on the end so you can go if it's bad."

"Thank you."

"And Ms Delight?"

"Yes, Bobby."

"You can say 'I don't know yet,'" said Bobby. "In the talking bit. If somebody asks you what you made of it. That's a real answer, you're allowed, we let everybody say it. Tomas says it every week. It's basically his thing now."

Ms Delight sat on a low wall outside a rec hall and looked at a small pink bear.

"I know," she said. "I invented it. I was nine."

Here is Playcare a year on, because I promised at the start that I would tell you what happened and not just what it felt like.

Ordinance 11 is gone and nobody has proposed anything like it since, though a member of the council did raise something about the ball pit in March and was voted down eight to one, which people found funny for weeks.

The Eight O'Clock Programme runs on Tuesdays and Thursdays. Attendance is about four hundred, which is down from eleven hundred, which Fenn says in her log is "exactly right and exactly what I wanted and I would like it noted that I have stopped apologising for it." Four hundred children who want to be there is a programme. Eleven hundred children who have nowhere else to be is a queue.

They do not only watch SML. They watch a lot of things. The most argued-about session of the entire first year was about a nature documentary and it nearly came to blows.

The handwritten books are gone. All of them. There was no order about it and nobody collected them in; they simply stopped being interesting the moment the thing they were a substitute for came back, and by October the lending library had folded, and the eleven-year-old who kept the ledger was extremely put out about it, because she had been running a functioning institution and now she was not.

Jacob still has Exhibit 34 and will not throw it away and will not let anybody read it.

The photocopier browser was disabled in August. It took the technician four minutes.

Priya Nandakumar is twelve now and writes things down on purpose. She is doing an oral history of the whole business, properly, with a recorder, and she has interviewed a hundred and eleven people, and she interviewed me for two and a half hours and corrected me nine times, and about a third of what is in this book is in it because of her.

CatNap still keeps his book. The number is back down to about fifty a week, which he says is roughly normal, and slightly better than before all this started, and he does not know why and has stopped trying to work it out.

Fenn is still Coordinator. She has been offered a promotion twice and has said no twice.

Marlow Delight is thirteen and does not watch it and goes on Tuesdays anyway and, since about February, argues. He argues extremely well. His mother says watching him do it is the strangest experience of her life.

Gus is ten and still reads at the table.

Otto is seven and has not changed in any respect whatsoever.

Mr Voss went back to the administration. He sent Jacob a letter in August. It said, in its entirety: "Mr Reyes — When you are older, come and work for me. I have been on the other side of you once and I do not wish to repeat it. V."

Jacob has it framed. It is the only thing he has ever taken out of the shoebox permanently.

Ada Olumide still lives four doors down from Ms Delight. They have tea on Sundays. They did not before.

And Ms Delight is in Room 4, where she has always been, teaching fractions to twenty-six children, with her permanent smile and her green notebook and three plants on the windowsill that are still doing well.

She goes to the Tuesdays.

She sits on the end of a row, in the third sitting, in the seat that Bobby BearHug saves for her, and she does not say very much, and about once a month somebody asks her what she made of it and she says "I don't know yet," and about four hundred children say it back to her in unison, which started as a joke in November and is now, apparently, a tradition.

She has a new photograph on her desk. It is turned so that visitors can see it.

It is a photograph of a girl about nine years old, sitting cross-legged on a carpet in a dark room, about three feet from a television, with her hair up and pinned in a way that clearly required decisions.

Jacob gave it back to her on the last day of the hearing. He did not have to; it had been entered as an exhibit and exhibits go into the register.

He copied it, put the copy in the register, and gave her the original, and when Ada Olumide found out about this three weeks later she wrote him a short note that said "That was improper and I would have allowed it. A.O."

Underneath the photograph, on her desk, Ms Delight keeps a card.

On the card, in round careful handwriting, it says:

ask the hard question

then sit there

Bobby BearHug wrote that.

She wrote it on about four hundred cards, actually, over about a month, and gave one to every adult in Playcare, because she is nine — ten, now — and because when Bobby BearHug decides that a thing needs saying she does not make a speech about it, she simply says it four hundred times until it is everywhere, which is, when you think about it, exactly the technique that got everybody into this mess in the first place.

That is how it works, apparently.

Something says a thing at you nine hundred times and it goes into you like a nail.

The only question anybody in Playcare ever had to answer — the whole thing, the entire seven months, the ordinance and the Wardens and the books and the eleven days in the old canteen hall and the girl on the carpet — was ever only about who gets to do the saying, and whether they stay in the room afterward.

Bobby BearHug counted thirty hugs before breakfast this morning.

I never told you the word.

I said at the start that I would not, and I have not, and we have come two hundred and sixty-odd pages and you have survived it, which was rather the point.

Here is the thing I want to leave you with.

Somewhere in the Schoolhouse there is a filing cabinet, and in the filing cabinet there is a folder, and in the folder there is a single sheet of paper dated the fifteenth of September, and on that sheet, in Ms Delight's handwriting, in quotation marks, is the word Bobby BearHug said when she knocked her pencil cup off her desk.

I have seen it. It is four letters. It is not "duck."

It is also, I promise you, completely and entirely boring.

That is the whole joke of this book and I have been waiting forty-four chapters to tell it to you.

Eleven hundred children lost an hour a day for seven months. Four hundred and ninety-three of them sat in a room with no windows. A woman who had done nothing but write a paper had her name attached to a court case for the rest of her life. A Warden resigned. A cat could not do his job. Two hundred and six pages of signatures went into a folder, and a hundred and nine documents went into a box, and nine hundred people sat in a canteen for eleven days.

And at the bottom of all of it — underneath the ordinance and the hearing and the ruling and the repeal — there is a nine-year-old bear, on a Tuesday morning, dropping some pencils.

Words are tools, Ms Delight said, on the board, at nine-fourteen in the morning, thirty-eight minutes before any of this started. A hammer can build a birdhouse and a hammer can break a window and the hammer does not know the difference. That is your job.

She was right.

She was right about that and she was right about almost everything else and she still got it wrong, and the reason she got it wrong is not that she was stupid or cruel or a hypocrite. She got it wrong because she was tired, and because the right answer took twenty years and she needed one by Thursday.

There isn't a rule that does it for you. There has never been one. There is not going to be one.

There is only ever somebody sitting down next to you on the carpet and asking what you made of it, and waiting, properly, all the way through the silence, for you to say:

"I don't know yet."
