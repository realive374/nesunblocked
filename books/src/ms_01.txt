@CH|1|A Perfectly Ordinary Morning in Playcare
Playcare woke up the way it always did, which is to say all at once and far too loudly.

The big lamps in the ceiling came on in their slow amber way, pretending to be a sunrise. The grass got its morning mist. Somewhere over by the ball pit, a whistle blew, and eleven hundred kids came pouring out of the dormitory halls like somebody had tipped over a bucket of them.

Bobby BearHug was already awake. Bobby BearHug was always already awake.

She was sitting on the low wall outside the Schoolhouse with her feet swinging, pink as a strawberry milkshake, counting the kids as they went past and giving out hugs to anybody who slowed down enough to get one. She had given out nine hugs before breakfast. That was a slow morning for Bobby. On a good morning she could break thirty.

"Morning, Bobby!"

"Morning, Tess!" Bobby held out her arms. Tess ran into them without breaking stride, got squeezed, and kept going.

Ten.

Across the green, DogDay was doing his stretches, which he did every single morning in full view of everybody, and which nobody had ever asked him to stop doing. Hoppy Hopscotch was already three laps into something. CraftyCorn had set up her easel facing the wrong direction on purpose, because she said she was painting the light and not the thing. KickinChicken went by on his board, going much too fast, and yelled something that might have been a greeting and might have been a warning.

PickyPiggy was standing at the breakfast window holding a tray and having a very serious conversation with the lunch lady about whether toast counted as a food or a container.

And CatNap was asleep in the crook of the big slide, purple and folded up like a dropped sock, because CatNap was asleep at every hour that was not directly adjacent to bedtime.

This, more or less, was a normal morning in Playcare.

If you have read the first four books, you already know that normal in Playcare has a short shelf life. You know about the week the Critters all woke up in each other's bodies and DogDay spent four days trying to sleep standing up. You know about the Great Switcheroo, and the second Great Switcheroo, and the one everybody agreed not to call the third Great Switcheroo even though that is obviously what it was.

So when I tell you that this was a perfectly ordinary morning, understand that I am telling you it was the last one for a while.

The bell rang.

It was not a nice bell. Playcare had a lot of nice things in it — the slides, the murals, the ball pit that was so deep that a rumor had gone around for two years that there was a whole second Playcare at the bottom of it — but the Schoolhouse bell was not one of them. It was flat and hard and it went right through you, and every single kid in Playcare had a physical reaction to it.

Bobby hopped off the wall.

"Come on," she said to nobody in particular, and about six kids fell in behind her, because that was the effect Bobby had on a crowd.

Inside, the Schoolhouse smelled like pencils and floor wax and the particular dusty warmth of a room that has been full of children for a very long time. The hallway was lined with cubbies. Above the cubbies was a poster that said BE KIND, BE CURIOUS, BE QUIET, and somebody had scratched out the third one about a year ago and nobody had ever fixed it, and this had become a small ongoing situation between the students and the administration.

Room 4 was at the end of the hall.

Bobby liked Room 4. She liked the big windows that looked out at nothing, and the map of a world she had never seen, and the reading corner with the beanbag that had lost most of its beans. She liked her desk, which was in the second row, and she liked her desk neighbor, a small serious kid named Jacob who kept his pencils in order of length and had once explained to Bobby, unprompted and at great length, the difference between a rule and a law.

She did not exactly like Ms Delight.

That is not quite right. It is more accurate to say that liking Ms Delight was a complicated job, and Bobby, who liked absolutely everybody, found it to be the single hardest piece of work in her entire day.

Ms Delight was already standing at the front of the room when they came in. She was always already standing at the front of the room. Nobody in Room 4 had ever once seen her arrive or leave, which was the kind of thing you noticed at nine years old and then decided very firmly not to think about.

She was tall. She wore a neat green dress with a white collar, and her hair was pinned up in a way that had clearly required decisions. And she was smiling.

Ms Delight was always smiling. That was the thing about her. The smile did not go anywhere. It did not get bigger when something was funny or smaller when something was not. It sat on her face exactly the same in every weather, and after a while you stopped reading it as a smile at all and started reading it as a kind of weather report for a place you could not visit.

"Good morning, Room Four," said Ms Delight.

"Good morning, Ms Delight," said Room 4.

"Sit, please."

They sat.

Bobby slid into her seat and got her notebook out and put her hands flat on the desk the way you were supposed to. Beside her, Jacob squared up his pencils. In front of her, a kid named Omar was already drawing on his wrist.

"Today," said Ms Delight, turning to the board, "we are going to talk about words."

Which, looking back on it, is one of the funniest things that has ever happened in Playcare. Nobody laughed at the time. Nobody knew to.

Ms Delight picked up a piece of chalk.

"Words," she said, "are tools. A hammer is a tool. A hammer can build a birdhouse and a hammer can break a window, and the hammer does not know the difference. The hammer does not care. That is your job."

She wrote the word TOOL on the board and underlined it twice.

"Some of you," she went on, and here the smile did not change at all but something behind it shifted, the way a house settles, "have been bringing hammers into my classroom."

Nobody moved.

"I have heard things in that hallway this week that I did not care for."

Twenty-six children found something extremely interesting about the surface of their desks.

"I am not going to name anybody. I am going to say this once. There are words we use in Room Four, and there are words we do not, and the list of words we do not is short and every one of you knows it by heart." She set the chalk down, very gently, which was somehow worse than slamming it. "Do we understand each other?"

"Yes, Ms Delight."

"Wonderful," said Ms Delight, and turned back to the board, and the lesson began.

Bobby wrote TOOL in her notebook and drew a small heart next to it, because Bobby drew a small heart next to most things.

It was 9:14 in the morning.

The word would happen at 9:52.
@CH|2|The Word
Here is the part where I have to be careful.

I am not going to tell you the word.

I want to be straight with you about that up front, so you are not sitting there for the next two hundred pages waiting for me to slip. I know what it was. Everybody in Room 4 knows what it was. It is written down in a folder in a filing cabinet in an office in the Schoolhouse, in Ms Delight's handwriting, in quotation marks, and I have seen the folder.

But this is not that kind of book, and anyway the word is the least interesting thing in this whole story. What happened because of the word is the interesting part. The word itself is just the match. Nobody remembers the match.

So here is what I will tell you. It was four letters long. It was not "duck." It was the kind of word that adults say when they drop something heavy on their foot and then look around quickly to see who heard. It was, in the grand scheme of bad words, somewhere in the middle — not the worst one, not close to the worst one, but comfortably, unarguably on the list.

And at 9:52 in the morning on an ordinary Tuesday, Bobby BearHug said it at a volume that could be described, generously, as a shout.

Here is how it happened.

They had moved on from words to fractions, which is a thing that happens in school, where you spend forty minutes on one subject and then a bell goes off inside the teacher and suddenly you are doing something completely different. Ms Delight had drawn a pie on the board and cut it into eighths and was explaining that three eighths and five eighths make a whole pie, which everybody already knew, because everybody in that room had at some point in their life divided a pie.

Bobby was not really listening. Bobby was drawing.

She had been working on the drawing all morning. It was a picture of all eight Critters standing in a row holding hands, which Bobby drew roughly once a week and never got tired of. She had done DogDay and Hoppy and Bubba and CraftyCorn and PickyPiggy and KickinChicken and CatNap, and she was on herself, which she always saved for last, and she had just gotten to the arms.

And she reached for her purple pencil.

And her elbow caught the edge of her pencil cup.

And the pencil cup went off the desk.

Now. You have to understand the physics of this. A pencil cup does not simply fall. A pencil cup enters the air and becomes a machine for distributing pencils across the largest possible area. It hit the floor on its rim and went over, and eleven pencils and a ruler and a pencil sharpener shaped like a strawberry all went in eleven different directions at once, and the sound it made in a completely silent classroom was roughly the sound of a small building coming down.

Every head in Room 4 turned.

And Bobby BearHug, staring down at the wreckage of her pencil cup, with her drawing half finished and her purple pencil rolling away under Omar's chair, said the word.

Loudly.

Clearly.

With feeling.

The thing about the way she said it was that she did not say it like a kid trying out a bad word. There was no giggle in it. She did not glance around to see if anybody had heard. She said it the way you say a word you have heard a thousand times, the way you say "ouch," automatically, like a reflex, the exact same way an adult says it when they drop something heavy on their foot.

That was what made it so bad.

That was what made twenty-six children go absolutely rigid in their chairs.

Because a kid saying a bad word on purpose is a small thing. Every teacher in the world has dealt with a kid saying a bad word on purpose. It is practically a ceremony. You say the word, the teacher does the face, you sit in the hall, everybody moves on.

But a kid saying a bad word by accident — a kid saying it so smoothly, so naturally, so completely without thinking about it, that it is obviously just part of how that kid talks now —

That is a different thing.

That is a thing a teacher wants to know the source of.

Bobby put her hand over her mouth.

It was the slowest, most horrible movement any of them had ever seen. Her eyes had gone very wide and very round. Her hand came up in stages, like it was arriving from a long way off, and when it got to her mouth she pressed it flat and held it there.

Nobody breathed.

Jacob, beside her, had gone completely still with a pencil in his hand. He told me later that his first thought was not "oh no." His first thought — and he was embarrassed about this afterward, and told me about it anyway, because Jacob was congenitally unable to leave a fact out of a story — his first thought was: I need to remember exactly what happens next. Every part of it.

Which turned out to be the single most useful thought anybody had in Playcare that entire year.

At the front of the room, Ms Delight had stopped mid-sentence with a piece of chalk touching the board.

She did not turn around right away.

That was the part the kids talked about for months afterward. The not turning around. She stood there with her back to the room for what Omar swore was a full ten seconds and what Jacob, who counted, said was four, and in that time the only sound in Room 4 was the strawberry pencil sharpener finishing its journey across the floor and coming to rest against the leg of the bookshelf.

Then she set the chalk in the tray.

Then she turned around.

And she was smiling, of course. She was always smiling. But there are a lot of different silences a smile can sit on top of, and every kid in that room understood, instantly and without being told, that this was a new one.

"Bobby," said Ms Delight.

Bobby's hand was still over her mouth.

"Bobby, put your hand down, please. I can't understand you with your hand there."

Bobby had not been saying anything. But she put her hand down.

"Thank you." Ms Delight's head tilted, about four degrees. "Would you repeat that for me?"

A small sound came out of Bobby.

"I'm not being cruel, Bobby. I want to be certain I heard you correctly. It would be very unfair of me to be upset about something I misheard. So." The tilt again. "Would you repeat it?"

"No, ma'am," Bobby whispered.

"No?"

"No, ma'am. I don't — " Bobby swallowed. "I don't want to say it again."

And here is a strange thing, and it is the first strange thing in this story, and if you are the kind of reader who likes to keep a list, this is where the list starts.

Ms Delight nodded.

"Good," she said.

Then she said: "That is exactly the right answer, and I want everyone in this room to notice that Bobby gave it." She let that sit for a second. "A word you will not repeat is a word you already know the size of."

Twenty-six children stared at her.

"Bobby, get your things, please. We're going to have a conversation in my office."

"Am I — " Bobby's voice came out very small. "Am I in trouble?"

"You're going to my office," said Ms Delight. "Those aren't the same. Get your things."

@CH|4|The Office
Ms Delight's office was not what Bobby expected.

Bobby had expected something like the nurse's room — small, white, a bit medical, with a chair you sat in while somebody decided about you. She had heard about the office for years. Every kid in Playcare had heard about the office. The office was where you went, and the going was the whole story, and nobody who had actually been inside it ever gave a satisfying description afterward, which had allowed the rumor to grow in the usual way. Omar's version had a filing cabinet with a lock on it. Priya's version had a window that looked out on somewhere real.

The office was, in fact, a small warm room with a rug.

There was a desk with a green lamp on it. There were two chairs, a big one and a smaller one, and the smaller one had a cushion. There was a bookshelf, absolutely stuffed, with the books shoved in at every angle like somebody had given up on the system a long time ago. There were plants — three of them, real ones, doing well.

And there was a picture on the desk, turned so that a visitor could not quite see it.

"Sit down," said Ms Delight. "The little one. It's more comfortable, and don't argue with me about it, everybody argues with me about it and everybody is wrong."

Bobby sat in the little one.

It was more comfortable.

Ms Delight walked home at 4:40 like she did every day.

Playcare had a staff village on the east side, past the orchard, where the ceiling came down lower and the lamps were warmer and there were about forty small houses in rows with actual front doors and actual gardens. It was nice. It was genuinely nice. People forget that part.

She walked past the orchard and she did not look at it.

She walked past Mrs Okonkwo, who was doing something to her hedge, and Mrs Okonkwo said "Evening," and Ms Delight said "Evening," in exactly her normal voice, and Mrs Okonkwo said afterward that there had been absolutely nothing unusual about her at all, which tells you something about Ms Delight and possibly also something about hedges.

She got to number 14.

She stood on the step for a second with her key in her hand.

Inside, she could hear the television.

Now, there was nothing strange about that. There was a television on in that house most evenings. Ms Delight was not one of those parents who pretended her house did not have a television in it; she thought those parents were liars, mostly, and she had said so, out loud, at a staff meeting, which was one of the six times anybody had ever heard her be funny.

What was strange was the sound the television was making.

It was making a screaming sound.

Not a scary screaming sound. Not a movie scream. A specific, particular, cartoonish, adult-male, comedic screaming sound, very loud, going on for much longer than screaming normally goes on for, and under it, layered over it, coming through the door in three separate registers —

Her children. Laughing.

All three of them. Marlow, who was twelve, and who had not laughed at anything Ms Delight had said or done in approximately fourteen months. Gus, who was nine. And Otto, who was six, and who was doing the high wheezing thing he did when he could not breathe properly from laughing, which was a sound Ms Delight loved more than almost any sound in the world.

She stood on her own front step and listened to her three children laughing harder than she had heard them laugh in a year.

She did not go in for a minute and a half.

I know that because Mrs Okonkwo, still doing something to her hedge, watched the whole thing, and mentioned it to somebody, who mentioned it to somebody, which is how everything in the staff village worked. She said Ms Delight just stood there with the key out. She said she thought maybe the lock was sticking.

Then Ms Delight put the key in the lock and opened the door and went inside and said, in the warmest voice she had:

"I'm home! What are we watching?"

And the television went off so fast that the click and the "nothing!" arrived at the same instant.
